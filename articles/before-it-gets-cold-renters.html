Nine things to do before it gets cold, for renters

From the Renter's Almanac. Updated September 2026.

Homeowners can add insulation, patch a roof or swap a furnace. Renters mostly cannot, and that is fine. A handful of small jobs, most of them cheap and none of them permanent, decide whether the first cold snap is a nuisance or an emergency. Here are nine, in the order we would tackle them.

Why the first hard freeze matters

A hard freeze is a night when the temperature stays at or below 28°F for several hours. It is the point at which weak seals, tired heating systems and exposed pipes get found out. In much of the Midwest the first one lands somewhere between late October and mid-November, and it rarely gives a week's warning. Heating technicians and repair crews book up within days of the first cold night, so the people who called early are the ones who get a visit.

How a home loses heat

A building loses heat in three ways. Air leaks in through gaps around windows and doors, which is called infiltration. Heat also conducts straight through glass and thin walls, and an old single-pane window can lose several times more than a wall of the same size. Finally, warm air rises and escapes through the top of the building while cold air is pulled in at the bottom. For a renter the lesson is simple: the cheapest gains are at the windows and doors, and a heating system that runs cleanly wastes less of what it makes.

The nine jobs

  1. Test your smoke and CO detectors and change the batteries before Nov 1. Press the test button on every detector, replace the batteries, and look at the date stamped on the back: any unit older than ten years should be replaced. Heating season is also carbon monoxide season, so make sure there is a CO alarm near the bedrooms.
  2. Get the furnace or heating unit serviced or inspected by mid-October. A technician will check the burner, filter, flue and thermostat in about an hour. Book the visit now, because the schedule fills quickly once the weather turns. Your lease will say who arranges it.
  3. Put insulation film on drafty windows before Nov 1. A shrink-film kit costs roughly $15 and covers several windows. Clean the frame, stick the tape, tack up the film, then warm it with a hair dryer until it pulls tight. It peels off in spring without marking the paint.
  4. Add a door draft stopper or sweep before Nov 1. Choose a removable one, such as a fabric snake or a stick-on sweep that peels clean. If you can slide a coin under the door, air is moving there all winter.
  5. Use space heaters safely. Buy only a UL-listed model, plug it directly into a wall outlet (never a power strip or extension cord), keep it at least three feet clear of curtains, bedding and other fabric, and never leave it running unattended.
  6. Report any window that will not seal or latch. A window that will not close tight is a repair, not a quirk. Report it in writing through whatever channel your lease names, attach a photo, and keep a copy of the message.
  7. Find the water shutoff and photograph it. Look under the kitchen sink, in the bathroom or in a utility closet, confirm the valve turns, and take a photo so you can find it fast in an emergency. A burst pipe floods a unit in minutes.
  8. Get the car ready. Replace streaky wiper blades, fill the washer reservoir with winter-rated fluid, and check the tires. Pressure drops about 1 psi for every 10°F the temperature falls, and tread depth matters more once roads get slick.
  9. Build a cold-weather kit. Keep a flashlight, spare batteries, a couple of blankets and a charger or power bank together in one bin near the door, so you are not searching in the dark if the power or heat goes out.
